# What makes thinking feel like progress and play: a literature sweep

Why this file exists: the protocol's planning gates feel like a chore. This sweep gathers the evidence on how humans prefer to think and to feel progress, so the protocol can make the journey enjoyable under its non-negotiable constraint — enjoyment must never cost output quality.

## 1. Flow and engagement

- Flow arises when challenges match skills, goals are clear, and feedback is immediate and unambiguous. The canonical nine characteristics (challenge–skill balance, merging of action and awareness, clear goals, unambiguous feedback, total concentration, sense of control, loss of self-consciousness, transformation of time, autotelic experience) are the textbook model. [CONFIRMED]
- Challenge–skill balance moderates performance, not just enjoyment: Engeser & Rheinberg (2008) show the balance predicts both flow and task performance, with perceived importance shifting the optimum. [LIKELY — peer-reviewed record verified, full text unfetched]
- Flow at work has antecedents and outcomes confirmed meta-analytically (2023 work-flow meta-analysis), and diary work links playful work design to flow and then to performance (JOOP 2025 diary study). Effect sizes are moderate and mostly correlational; causal evidence is thinner. [LIKELY]
- → Design implication: keep each gate inside the challenge–skill channel (neither trivial form-filling nor overwhelming blank-page design), state the goal of each gate in one sentence, and close the feedback loop inside the gate (verdicts, visible state changes), not sessions later.

## 2. Progress visibility and goal gradients

- The goal-gradient hypothesis (Hull, 1932): organisms expend disproportionately more effort as they approach a goal. Resurrected and replicated in modern consumer research (Kivetz et al. 2006, coffee-purchase acceleration; credit-repayment gradient replication). [CONFIRMED for existence and direction; primary full texts unfetched]
- The "progress principle" (Amabile & Kramer): of all things that drive inner work life, perceived progress in meaningful work is the strongest; small wins ignite joy, engagement, and creativity. Built on ~12,000 daily diary entries from knowledge workers. [CONFIRMED for the diary-study base and the book's claim; HBR full text paywalled and unfetched]
- The Zeigarnik picture has been revised against the naive reading. A 2025 meta-analysis (Ghibellini & Meier) found NO general memory advantage for unfinished over finished tasks — but a robust tendency to *resume* interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect holds; the memory claim does not). Meanwhile unfinished tasks intrude: they drive rumination and worse sleep (Syrek et al. 2017) and impair work recovery (2026 meta-analysis). [LIKELY — single meta-analysis via tertiary source, full text unfetched; the correction is load-bearing so it is flagged, not buried]
- → Design implication: open loops pull people back (good for return-to-work), but open loops also ruminate (bad for evenings). Protocol implication: every gate should *close visibly* — a checked-off, named win — while the *next* gate stays previewed but unstarted. Do not leave planners marinating in half-finished gates; that is the chore feeling plus the sleep cost.

## 3. Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness

- SDT's core claim: autonomy, competence, and relatedness are basic psychological needs; contexts that satisfy them foster volitional, high-quality motivation, better performance, persistence, and creativity, while thwarting them harms motivation and wellness. Mini-theories (Cognitive Evaluation Theory, Organismic Integration Theory) spell out how rewards, controls, and ego-involvements shift motivation along the autonomy–control continuum. [CONFIRMED — primary theory source plus decades of cited program: Ryan & Deci 2000; Deci & Ryan 1985/2000]
- At work specifically: SDT-based synthesis for organisations (Deci, Olafsen & Ryan 2017, Annual Review) and a meta-analysis of leader autonomy support confirm the pattern holds in workplaces, including for performance quality, not just satisfaction. [LIKELY — records verified, full texts unfetched]
- → Design implication: this is the master constraint on every enjoyment intervention. Autonomy (choice over how to work a gate), competence (gates pitched so the planner feels capable, with visible mastery), relatedness (the protocol as a collaborator, not an auditor). Anything that buys engagement by spending autonomy is a loan with interest (see §6).

## 4. Productive failure and desirable difficulty

- Productive failure (Kapur): attempting problems before instruction — and failing — produces deeper conceptual understanding and better transfer than direct instruction first, while matching it on basic knowledge; more failed attempts predict more learning; replicated across the USA, Canada, Germany, and Australia. The failure must be followed by real instruction that builds on it. [CONFIRMED for the effect pattern; the replication claim rests on Kapur's own first-person summary plus verified peer-reviewed records, full texts unfetched]
- Desirable difficulties (Bjork & Bjork): spacing, retrieval practice (testing effect), interleaving, and generation impair short-term performance while improving long-term learning — the learning-vs-performance distinction. A 2020 integrative review (JARMAC) confirms the family and its boundary conditions. [CONFIRMED]
- → Design implication, and a warning: difficulty that feels bad can be the mechanism — which argues AGAINST sanding every gate smooth. Keep generative struggle (draft the decision before reading guidance; attempt the decomposition before seeing the template), but pair every struggle with consolidation instruction and never mistake friction of the *form* (confusing gates, lost state) for friction of the *thought*. The first is desirable; the second is just a chore.

## 5. Play, exploration, and playfulness at work

- Playful work design (PWD) is a validated construct: employees can proactively redesign work to be more fun and competitive, with a validated scale (Scharp et al.), theoretical grounding (Bakker et al. 2020), and diary evidence linking PWD through engagement to performance. [LIKELY — records verified, full texts unfetched]
- The sharpest directly relevant finding: a WHU dissertation's experimental field study compared ludic (playful, intrinsic) vs agonistic (competitive, extrinsically rewarding) play interventions. Ludic designs fostered sustained motivation and well-being; agonistic designs could boost short-term performance but at the cost of relational strain. The same program documents the light AND dark sides of playful climates and the lived tension between fun, pressure, and performance. [LIKELY — single dissertation, abstract fetched in full, constructs and direction verified]
- The serious/playful tension is real and must be managed, not wished away: play at work faces a legitimacy problem (the "work of play" literature), and playful frames can read as unserious precisely where rigour matters. [LIKELY]
- → Design implication: design gates to be *ludic* (curiosity, exploration, self-expression, humour with a straight face) and never *agonistic* (no streaks, no scores, no comparisons). Protect the legitimacy: play frames the *process*, never the *stakes* — the gate still decides something real.

## 6. What does NOT work

- Expected, tangible, contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks; informational, non-controlling verbal rewards (positive feedback that supports competence without controlling) can enhance it. This is the Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) meta-analytic program and CET's core prediction. Boundary conditions are genuinely contested (commentaries by Eisenberger, Cameron, and others dispute scope), but the core undermining effect of expected tangible contingent reward on interesting tasks is one of the most replicated findings in the field. [CONFIRMED for the core effect; CONTESTED on boundaries — labelled as such]
- Gamification of knowledge work (points, badges, leaderboards): a review of gamification in science learning concludes the evidence for efficacy is inconclusive and insufficient (Frontiers, NRC 2011 lineage); effectiveness claims outrun the data. Combined with §5's agonistic-strain finding and §3's autonomy mechanism, competitive extrinsic overlays are the wrong tool for complex creative work. [LIKELY against naive gamification; the primary-review record is fetched and explicit]
- The specific claim "gamification helps simple tasks but hurts complex ones" is plausible and consistent with the above, but no direct source verifying that interaction was found in this sweep. [UNVERIFIED — open gap, do not design against it as fact]
- Dark patterns / engagement maximisation: manipulation taxonomies are catalogued (Brignull 2010 onwards: roach motels, confirmshaming, etc.; OECD taxonomy), and autonomy-undermining design is philosophically and empirically tied to experienced loss of agency. Under SDT (§3), engagement bought this way spends autonomy and will be paid back in motivation quality. [CONFIRMED that the patterns exist and are catalogued; the SDT linkage is synthesis]
- → Rules out: points, badges, leaderboards, streaks, social comparison, progress-extortion ("complete your profile"), any reward contingent on gate completion, and any engagement metric optimised for its own sake.

## 7. The chore/burden perception of documentation and process

- Documentation burden is a recognised, measured problem in the heaviest-documentation profession (healthcare scribing): burden-reduction is an active research target (Nature 2024 LLM-scribe review; JAMIA Open studies; "ticks" and Beyond-GROSS programs). The mechanism generalises via cognitive load theory: extraneous load (how information and tasks are *presented*) consumes the resources that germane load (actual thinking) needs; reducing presentation friction frees capacity for the real work. [LIKELY for the burden literature — records verified, full texts unfetched; CONFIRMED for the extraneous/germane distinction as textbook theory]
- Transfer caution: none of the burden literature studies software-planning protocols. That gate-chore resembles scribe-chore is synthesis, not evidence. [UNVERIFIED as transfer — stated plainly]
- → Design implication: attack *extraneous* load first (state carried between gates automatically, templates pre-filled with what is known, no re-asking, no lost context), because every unit of form-friction removed is a unit of thought-capacity returned. This is burden reduction without rigour reduction — the mechanism is load reallocation, not standard-lowering.

## 8. What this licenses / what it rules out

Strong enough to design against (CONFIRMED): satisfy autonomy/competence/relatedness in every gate; keep challenge in the skill channel with clear goals and immediate feedback; close gates visibly for small wins; never use expected tangible contingent rewards or competitive overlays; cut extraneous form-load before touching standards; use generative struggle followed by consolidation.

Usable with care (LIKELY): goal-gradient shaping (show nearness to done); open-loop previews for resumability with mandatory visible closure; ludic-not-agonistic play framing; flow-oriented gate sizing.

Ruled out: points/badges/leaderboards/streaks; engagement-maximising patterns; sanding away all difficulty; leaving gates half-open as a motivation tactic (the rumination cost exceeds the resumption benefit); citing the simple-vs-complex gamification interaction as fact.

Against the premise (honest counterweight): desirable difficulties *feel bad by design* — some chore-feeling is the learning mechanism, and removing it would cost quality; productive failure requires real follow-up instruction, not vibes; unfinished tasks impair recovery, so "compelling open loops" have a documented cost; agonistic play can raise short-term output while straining the humans — a trap for any metric that stops at throughput.
